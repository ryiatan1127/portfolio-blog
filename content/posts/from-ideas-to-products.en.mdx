I have always believed that the most interesting part of software engineering is not simply learning a programming language.

It is the moment when an idea becomes something that actually works.

When I first started learning computer science, programming was mostly about solving problems.

Write a program.

Pass the test cases.

Implement an algorithm.

Finish the assignment.

But as I learned more, I started asking myself a different question:

> **What can I build if nobody tells me what to build?**

That question gradually changed the way I approached computer science.

---

## From Learning to Building

I began participating in AI-related competitions and experimenting with technologies beyond the classroom.

More importantly, I started building my own projects.

Through these experiences, I gradually realized that I was less interested in simply learning a technology and more interested in discovering what I could create with it.

That became the foundation of my journey as a builder.

---

## Exploring AI Agents

Artificial intelligence became one of the areas I was most interested in.

Competitions gave me opportunities to explore machine learning and AI from a technical perspective.

But I wanted to go one step further.

I started thinking about how AI could become part of a complete software system.

A chatbot can answer questions.

An Agent, however, can potentially understand a task, use tools, interact with an environment, and complete a workflow.

That difference fascinated me.

So I started experimenting with my own Agent-based projects.

---

## Teaching an Agent to Build 2D Games

One of my projects is an Agent designed to help generate 2D games in batches.

The original idea was simple:

> If humans can build games with code, can an AI system participate in the process as well?

The implementation quickly showed me that game development is much more than writing code.

A game involves logic, scenes, assets, UI, interactions, configuration, testing, and debugging.

I therefore started breaking the process into smaller executable steps and designing workflows that allowed an Agent to work through them.

This project taught me an important lesson:

> **An open-ended goal has to be transformed into a structured workflow before an AI system can reliably execute it.**

That idea has influenced how I approach Agent development ever since.

---

## Making Automation Feel More Human

Another project I built was an automated Xiaohongshu workflow.

It connects information gathering, content processing, content creation, and publishing, while experimenting with more human-like interaction.

This project made me realize that automation is not simply about making a computer click faster.

Real websites are dynamic.

Elements move.

Pages change.

Workflows break.

Unexpected states appear.

Therefore, reliable automation requires much more than a sequence of clicks.

It requires thinking about:

* Browser automation
* Web interaction
* Workflow design
* State management
* Error handling
* Robustness

This was an important engineering lesson for me:

> **The difficult part is not making a system work once. It is making it work reliably.**

---

## Building Software for Real Problems

I also developed a multi-platform CRM aggregation system.

The idea came from a practical problem.

Many teams communicate through multiple platforms such as Telegram and WhatsApp.

When conversations and accounts are scattered across different platforms, managing them becomes inefficient.

I therefore explored how information from different platforms could be integrated into a unified system, including multi-account and multi-platform management.

This project changed the way I thought about software.

Technology itself is not the final goal.

**Solving a real problem is.**

That was another step in my transition from simply being a developer to becoming a builder.

---

## Software as a Creative Medium

Not all of my projects are productivity tools.

I also created **PRISON**, a text-based drag-and-drop puzzle game.

For this project, I was interested in a different question:

> **How can software create an experience?**

I explored interaction, puzzles, narrative, feedback, and the relationship between the player and the system.

It reminded me that software engineering is not only about algorithms and architecture.

It can also be a medium for storytelling, interaction, and creativity.

That combination is one of the reasons I enjoy computer science.

---

## ORDO

Another project I built is **ORDO**, a lightweight personal finance application.

Compared with my AI projects, ORDO taught me something different.

It made me think more deeply about product design.

Instead of asking:

> What features can I add?

I started asking:

> **What does the user actually need?**

These questions sound similar, but they lead to very different products.

The first focuses on the developer.

The second focuses on the user.

Through ORDO, I became increasingly interested in the idea that good software is not necessarily software with more features.

> **Good software makes the right things easier.**

---

## Looking Beyond Code

Looking back, my projects may seem quite different.

AI Agents.

Automation.

CRM.

Games.

Productivity software.

A personal blog.

But they are connected by one idea:

> **I like turning ideas into things that people can actually interact with.**

I enjoy starting with an unfinished idea, learning what I do not yet know, breaking a problem into smaller pieces, experimenting, failing, and eventually making something work.

That is probably one of the reasons I chose software engineering.

For me, software engineering is not simply about writing code.

It is a way of turning ideas into reality.

## Beyond Technology

Outside of technology, I have also tried to expose myself to a more international environment.

I participated in the Harvard Summit and student internship programs.

I have also received awards including the National First Prize in the Big Mouth English Competition and a Zhejiang Provincial First Prize in the 21st Century Cup English Speaking Competition.

These experiences taught me that technical ability and international communication are not separate skills.

A creative engineer needs to understand technology, but also people, cultures, and different ways of thinking.

That is why I want to continue exploring international opportunities.

I want to put myself into unfamiliar environments, meet people with different backgrounds, learn from different perspectives, and bring my own experiences into those conversations.

---

## Keep Building

I am still a software engineering student.

There is still a lot I do not know.

Many of my projects are far from perfect.

But I am becoming increasingly certain about one thing:

> **I want to keep building.**

Keep learning AI.

Keep exploring Agents.

Keep developing software.

Keep experimenting with games and products.

Keep documenting my growth.

And keep stepping outside familiar environments to discover how far technology — and I — can go.

For me, university is not about finding a final answer.

It is about continuing to ask better questions.

And perhaps the next answer is hidden inside the next idea that has not been built yet.
